What the Watchman Saw

By The Falconer · 23 November 2026

Prologue - The Wall

Pride begins with I, not with God, and every city that has ever burned burned first in the mind of one man who believed his name was worth more than his neighbour's life.

There is a city called Harrowmere, built upon a hill and divided by a single wall of grey stone, and on the one side of that wall stands the house of Thornwick and on the other stands the house of Ashgrave, two families of equal wealth and equal pride who have hated each other for so many years that not one living man can tell you where the hatred began, only that it was handed down to him at birth as a father hands down a sword, heavy and cold and already stained with the blood of men he never knew. He took it without a question because to question it would have meant to stand alone. A man in that city would rather die beside his own people than live beside the truth.

What is the quarrel, you ask, and I tell you there is no quarrel, there is only the memory of a quarrel. A memory fed daily by the ego is stronger than any fact, for the ego does not ask what was done, it asks only who is to blame. It always finds an answer. The answer is always the man across the wall, whilst the real enemy sits quietly in the chest of the man who is angry and laughs as he watches the two houses tear each other apart in the name of honour.

The prince of Harrowmere knows all of this, he is not a stupid man, three times he has stood in the market square with his guards behind him and warned the houses that the next blade drawn in his streets would cost the man who drew it his life, and three times the houses bowed their heads and swore obedience, and three times they went home and sharpened their steel, for obedience of the lips is cheap and obedience of the heart is the rarest thing under the sun, it cannot be forced by any law, only by the fear of God. The fear of God is exactly the thing that Harrowmere has forgotten.

But do not think this is a story about two wicked houses, for wicked men are easy to understand and easy to hate, and this is a harder story than that, this is a story about good people, about young hearts and old hearts and one gentle old man in a brown robe who all meant well and all walked, each by his own reasoning, straight into the fire, and if you can read it to the end without seeing your own face in one of them then perhaps you have not looked closely enough.

Above the city, on the ridge where the wind never stops, there is a watchman who sees the whole of it, the wall and the houses and the streets and the two children who will meet in the dark. He has watched it happen in a thousand cities and he has said what he could to the wind. The wind has kept every word. It is of him I will speak when the story is done.

For now, listen, and let me tell you what he saw.

Act I - The Thumb and the Feast

The body is temporary and the mind is forever, yet it is always the body that draws first, and always the pride that tells it to.

It began as it always begins, with nothing, at noon in the market of Harrowmere when two servants of the house of Ashgrave walked past two servants of the house of Thornwick and one of them lifted his thumb to his teeth, a small gesture, an old insult, so small that a wise man would have let it fall to the ground and forgotten it, but there were no wise men in that square, only men who had been taught from the cradle to hear every sentence as a sentence about themselves, and so the question came, did you bite your thumb at us. The answer came, I bit it. The steel came out before anyone could ask what the thumb had meant, because the ego does not need to know what was meant, it only needs to believe that it was meant for it.

Merrin, a Thornwick, a man made for peace, ran between the blades with his own sword held high, begging them to put up their steel and go home. It was the worst thing he could have said, for a man who is told he does not know what he is doing will always prove that he does. Then Garrick came, nephew of Ashgrave, a young man whose whole body was a drawn blade. He looked at Merrin's sword and laughed and said that he hated the word peace as he hated hell, as he hated every Thornwick alive, and with that the market became a field, the citizens ran with clubs, the old men screamed from their windows. The two patriarchs came limping out in their night robes with swords too heavy for them to lift, each held back by a wife who knew better than he did what was coming.

The prince rode in on a grey horse and the whole square fell silent, as men fall silent before a verdict, he did not shout, a man of real authority never needs to, he only said that the next blood spilled would be paid for in blood. The patriarchs bowed their heads. In their hearts they were already planning how to avoid the sentence without once planning to avoid the sin.

Now I want you to look at a young man named Caelan, only son of the house of Thornwick, who on that same morning was walking alone in the grove at the edge of the city with his hands behind his back and his eyes on the ground, awake all night and yet not tired, which is the exhaustion of those who think too much, in love, or so he believed, with a girl named Marenne who had sworn herself to God and would not so much as look at him. He sighed. He wrote. He wept without tears, and when his friend Merrin found him and asked what was wrong he said only that he was in love and out of her favour, and here is the first thing you must understand about him, he did not yet love anyone, he loved the shape of longing, he loved the ache itself, he had never once asked himself whether he wanted her or whether he merely wanted to want, and there was a second voice in that grove which he did not know was not his own, speaking softly in his own cadence, saying she has never thought of you, you are a pleasant sorrow to her, a story she tells her friends, why not take what is offered and call it enough. Caelan did not answer it. But he did not refuse it either, and that, Reader, is how every ruin begins.

It happened by accident, it always does, a servant of Ashgrave who could not read stopped Caelan and Merrin in the street and asked them to read aloud a list of names, it was the guest list for a feast to be held that very night in the red house, and among the names was Marenne. Caelan did not look up. The servant, grateful, told them to come to the feast as well if they were not of the house of Thornwick, having no idea who he spoke to, and Merrin said go, compare her face with other faces, and I will make you think your swan a crow. Caelan laughed for the first time in a week and went, not to forget her, only to look at her once more, which is the oldest reason there is and has led more men into more traps than any other.

Across the wall in a bright room at the top of the stairs a girl of thirteen was being laced into a white dress, her name was Aveline and she was the only daughter of Ashgrave. She did not yet know that her mother had been speaking with a nobleman named Davin about her marriage, she knew only what a child knows, the pleasure of a new gown and the heat of a crowded room and the slow wonder of being looked at for the first time as though she were someone, but mark what kind of girl she was, not a fragile one, a watchful one, women excel in the passions of sorrow and sympathy and self and Aveline had all three already, she felt what the room felt and kept most of it behind her eyes, and when the music began she came down the stairs slowly and watched before she was watched, as one who understood too young that a house is also a stage.

The doors opened and the masks came in under the torches, silk and feathers and laughter, and among them one plain mask belonging to a man who had no right to be there. He did not find Marenne, he found instead, through a gap in the dancers, a girl in white laughing at something her nurse had said, her head tilted, and her smile was not the practised smile of a young woman at her first feast, it was the real one, the one made before the mind remembers to be careful. Caelan stopped. The music went on. The dancers moved around him as water moves around a stone. He closed his eyes and saw the smile and opened them and it was still there, and for the first time in his life he did not think about himself, the ache that had followed him for weeks was gone as if it had never existed. In its place there was an attention so pure that it frightened him, for there is a second kind of pride and it says I am the one who suffers. It left him in the instant he saw her.

He crossed the hall and took her hand. He said that if he profaned this holy shrine with his unworthy hand then the gentle sin was his and he would mend it with a kiss. She answered, with a wit that surprised them both, that saints have hands which pilgrims may touch and that palm to palm was the only kiss a pilgrim needed. They spoke for a minute, perhaps two. In that time the whole feast faded around them. Then he kissed her, once and quickly, as one afraid of being seen. She kissed him back. It was the first thing either of them had ever done that belonged entirely to themselves.

The nurse arrived to say that her mother wanted her. Caelan asked who her mother was. The answer was the lady of the house. He went cold, and three minutes later Aveline asked the same nurse for the name of the young man who had left by the garden door and learned that he was Caelan, a Thornwick, the only son of her father's great enemy. She stood in the middle of the room and said what no one in that city had ever said, that her only love had sprung from her only hate, too early seen unknown and known too late.

Garrick had seen the plain mask and known the voice beneath it and called for his sword, he wanted blood on the floor of his uncle's house, but old Ashgrave, proud man but not a stupid one, forbade it, saying that he would not have a guest insulted under his roof whatever his name, and Garrick was made to be silent but not to be calm, for a man who is told to swallow his fury does not lose it, he stores it. It ripens, and I will tell you something about vows made in silence, they are the most binding of all, because there is no one left to release you from them.

The Watchman

On the ridge above Harrowmere the watchman looked down at the torches going out one by one in the red house and at a single figure standing in the garden under a balcony, not yet knowing what he would do. He had seen this before, a man looks at a woman and the world rearranges itself around her for one breath, he had seen it end in marriage and in murder and in the slow disappointment of two people who learn they were never what each other thought. He was not cynical, a watchman cannot afford to be, he knew the thing was real and the nearest a man comes to the sight of God. He knew also that it is the thing pride most readily wears as a mask. He said to the night, be careful, it is the best thing you will ever feel and the worst thing that can happen to you, do not confuse the two, and no one heard him, they never do.

Act II - The Wall and the Garden

Two things are held in two hands, the desire to be loved and the desire to be good, and most men drop the one in order to hold the other.

The feast ended and the torches were put out and the red house went dark. Caelan did not go home, he walked away from his friends and the street to the high wall that ran around the orchard of Ashgrave, a wall that was not tall as walls go, a man could climb it with a little effort, but a wall is not measured in stone, Reader, it is measured in what lies on the other side of it and what it would cost to cross. He told himself that his heart was already there and his body only followed it like a servant follows a master. He climbed, whilst behind him Lucan, a loud laughing man who turned every sorrow into a joke so that no one would see it was also his own, called out into the dark that Caelan was a lover and a madman and a sigh in the shape of a boy. Caelan did not answer, and Lucan gave up and went to his bed. Caelan said quietly to no one that he jests at scars who never felt a wound, which was true. A man who has never felt a wound is also a free man, though Lucan did not know it yet. It was the last thing he would ever have.

A window opened on the upper floor and a light came on and in the light stood a girl in a white gown with her hair loose, leaning on her hand, thinking aloud as the young do when they are sure no one can hear. It was not a thing Caelan should have been listening to, a man of honour would have walked away. He did not walk away, a hunger had begun in him that was stronger than honour and he did not yet have the discipline to name it. Aveline spoke to the empty night, asking why he had to be Caelan, asking him to refuse his father and give up his name, or if he would not then only to swear himself to her and she would no longer be an Ashgrave. She said it without a tremble in her voice, for this was not a girl playing at love, this was someone who had already looked at the problem from every side in the hours since the feast and had found nowhere to put it down, what is a name, she said, it is not a hand or a foot or an arm or a face or any other part of a man, a rose by any other word would smell as sweet.

Notice what she was doing, she was dividing a man from his name and the name from the blood and the blood from the hatred, she was performing in her own mind the surgery that no one in the whole city had ever dared to attempt, it was the most intelligent thing anyone in Harrowmere had done in a generation. She was thirteen and alone and believed that no one could hear. Caelan could not stay silent, he stepped out of the shadow and said he would take her at her word, call him love and he would be born again and never be Caelan any more. She started and her hand went to her mouth and she asked what man he was that stumbled so on her private thoughts in the night.

They spoke low because the walls had ears and the house had servants, she asked how he had come and why, since the orchard walls were high and the place was death if any of her kinsmen found him. He said that love had lent him wings and that no stone wall could keep love out. She was afraid, and not for herself. He said there was more danger in her eyes than in twenty of their swords. It is easy to be swept away by a sentence like that, it is easy to write it down and call it romance, but Reader, look closer, for what is being said beneath it is that he would rather die in her sight than live outside it, a gift and a trap, the language of a man who has stopped being able to imagine any life but one, and she, to her credit, heard the trap, a watchful one, she was always watchful, and asked if he loved her, saying she knew he would say yes and she would take his word, but lovers swear falsely and the gods are said to laugh at it, and when he began to swear by the moon she stopped him, the inconstant moon that changes every month, lest his love prove as changeable, and said do not swear at all, or if you must then swear by your own self, which is the god of my idolatry, and I will believe you.

Here the story must pause, because here is the first small crack. A narrator must be honest enough to name it, the god of my idolatry, she said it lightly, almost as a joke, but there is a thing a person must never say to another person however lightly, that they are a god, it is not a compliment, it is the oldest lie, the first temptation, the original whisper in the garden, you shall be as gods, and two children were about to give each other what only God can give, and neither of them knew the cost, for the pride which began with the word I does not always arrive dressed as pride, sometimes it arrives dressed as love, in the shape of the most beautiful thing in the world.

The nurse called from within and Aveline had to go, but before she went she said that if his love was honourable and his purpose marriage then he should send word tomorrow by one she would send to him, saying where and when he would perform the rite. She did not say love me, she said marry me, a child more serious than every adult in the city, wanting a vow and a witness and a name for what was happening and a place in the order of things, wanting what the old world gave to those who were afraid, a promise made in front of God. She went in, and came out, and went in again. Caelan stood in the orchard a long time looking at the closed window. The second voice, the soft one that spoke in his own cadence, said is this not the greatest thing that has ever happened to you, is there anything the world could offer you beyond this, you do not need God, you do not need your father, you do not need the whole broken city, you have found the thing that makes it all unnecessary. He did not answer it. But he did not refuse it either.

When the grey light came he was in a small stone cell at the edge of the city on his knees beside an old man in a brown habit, Brother Anselm, a gardener and herbalist who had spent forty years learning which plants heal and which plants kill and learning the unsettling truth that it is often the same plant at a different dose. The old man asked if he had been with Marenne. Caelan said he had forgotten that name and that name's woe. The old man stared at him, for a week ago the boy had come weeping about her, and now he asked to be married to an Ashgrave. He said that young men's love lies not truly in their hearts but in their eyes. He was right, the friar is always right in this story. It is his tragedy, because being right is not the same as being heard.

But he was also an old man who had watched this feud eat his city from the inside, he had married and buried both houses. In his heart against all his better judgement something leapt, the faint dangerous hope of a gardener who has seen a weed flower. He agreed to help, hoping that the union might turn the rancour of the two houses into peace, and here, Reader, is the hinge on which the whole story turns, the friar had a good motive, not a bad one or a selfish one, he wanted an end to the killing. He agreed to do it in secret, and there is a kind of sin that does not look like sin, the sin of the right thing done the wrong way, of the good end chosen over the good road, he did not ask the fathers, he did not ask the prince, he did not truly ask God, he only offered Him the finished plan, and as he turned to prepare the altar he said the sentence the whole city would later wish he had listened to, wisely and slow, they stumble that run fast, and then, because he was human, he ran.

The nurse was sent to find Caelan and found Lucan first, who had not slept and who could never resist an old woman in a large hat, and made a spectacle of her in the street, singing at her and calling her names not worth repeating. She demanded some respect with great and wounded dignity, and let us not be comic about her, she was a woman who had nursed Aveline at her own breast, whose own daughter died the day Aveline was weaned, who had spent thirteen years pouring every unused piece of love into the child of a family that did not know her name, more love than anyone in the house and no standing at all, and when she found Caelan she gave her message in her own roundabout way, complaining of her bones and her back and the heat and the slowness of the young. He bore it with patience, the first time in the whole story that this boy had been patient for another's sake. He told her to say that at noon in the friar's cell she would be married. He gave her a coin, and as she left she said, almost in passing, a thing that should have been a warning, two may keep a secret if one of them is away. A secret is a thing that two can keep, it becomes a different thing at three, at four it becomes a story, and at five it becomes a weapon.

At noon in a small stone chapel that smelled of wax and cold, two children knelt before an old man in a brown robe, no guests, no music, no father to give the bride away and no mother to weep, only the old words which his order had said over every Harrowmere child for three hundred years and the low voices of the two answering one after the other as the stone walls threw them back. They were married, and I will say one thing about this and I will say it once, it was a real marriage, witnessed and blessed and binding before God, and nothing that follows will undo it, whatever else this story is it is not about two children who played at love, they took a vow and the vow was kept and the vow is the reason the ending will be as terrible as it is, a man can be unfaithful to a feeling but he cannot be unfaithful to a promise, not without cost. The friar frowned and said that violent delights have violent ends and die in their own triumph like fire and powder, which as they kiss consume, therefore love moderately, long love does so, but Aveline came forward light as a feather and took her husband's hand and did not hear him, the young never do. The old are left to have been right.

The Watchman

The watchman saw the little chapel empty and the two small figures running in opposite directions across the roofs, each toward a house that was not theirs, he had said what he had to say and the wind had kept it, and below him a servant of Thornwick wiped a table and a servant of Ashgrave carried water to a cistern and neither knew that tonight in a locked room at the top of a staircase the two houses were being quietly joined. He said, I will tell you what is wrong with a secret, it is not the hiding, it is that the hidden thing must be defended and a thing that must be defended is always at war, they have married in peace and they have begun a war. He was not angry, watchmen do not get angry, they get tired. He turned his eyes to the sun going down on the wrong side of the world and watched the dark begin to climb the streets, tomorrow was a bad day, he knew it by the heat.

Act III - The Blade at Noon

Brotherhood and honour and order are the virtues of men, but without the fear of God they become the three best reasons to kill.

It was the hottest day of the year and the sun stood over the square like a held breath and the stones threw the heat back into the faces of those who walked on them, nobody slept, the dogs lay in the shade of the fountain, the old men sat on their doorsteps with their shirts open and said nothing, and Reader, notice the weather, for a story's weather is never an accident, when the day is this hot the blood is hot, and hot blood is a short fuse, and Merrin felt it first as he walked through the market with Lucan and stopped and begged that they retire, for the Ashgraves were abroad and the mad blood of these hot days was stirring and if they met there would be a brawl, he was the wise one, the quiet one, a man who had tried to keep the peace since the first scene and never once succeeded and knew it and went on trying, which is a kind of courage that no one writes songs about, and Lucan laughed and said he was like those fellows who walk into a tavern and lay their sword upon the table praying God send me no need of thee and by the second cup draw on the serving boy for nothing. He was describing himself and did not know it, the most dangerous kind of joke, the one that is also a confession, and Garrick came across the square.

He had not slept either, he had been awake since the feast turning the face of one young man over in his mind, a Thornwick who had walked into his uncle's house in a mask and laughed and left, an insult he could not name. A man who cannot name his insult will go on inventing new reasons to be insulted until the grievance becomes a character. He walked up to Lucan and Merrin with two companions and a smile that had nothing in it and asked for a word, and Lucan said make it a word and a blow, and Garrick said he would find him ready if he were given occasion, and Lucan asked could he not take occasion without being given it. That is the whole feud in three lines, one man says give me a reason and the other says I do not need one, and notice what had happened, not one man in that street was angry about anything that had actually occurred, they were angry about a thing said by their grandfathers and by their grandfathers' grandfathers, handed down through the blood like an inheritance no one remembers accepting, a kind of possession, the second voice again, speaking not into one ear but into two houses, you are insulted, you must answer, honour demands it, if you do not they will say you are afraid, no one said it aloud, there was no need, it was in the heat. Then a young man came into the square walking quickly with the unguarded face of someone who has been smiling for hours and cannot stop. It was Caelan.

Garrick saw him and his whole body changed, here comes my man, he said. He called him a villain, not a clever word, not even a rich one, a plain word spat out with the contempt a man keeps for those he has already decided to kill, and what happened next is the most extraordinary thing in the whole story and nobody remarks on it, Caelan did not answer, here was a young man in a hot square in front of his friends called a villain by the best swordsman in Harrowmere and every rule of his world said he must draw, every voice in his blood said answer him. He stood with his hands at his sides and said gently that the reason he had to love Garrick excused the rage of such a greeting, that he was no villain. That Garrick did not know him, which was true, for as of noon the day before he was Garrick's kinsman, he had married his cousin. The hatred could no longer reach him because something larger had taken its place.

This is the second hand, Reader, in one hand he held a vow he could not speak and in the other a name he could not defend. He chose mercy. It was the right choice and the wrong time, for in a world where honour has no meaning without the blade mercy looks like cowardice, and Garrick did not see a man who had been changed, he saw a man who had been beaten, and Garrick said that this would not excuse the injuries he had done him, turn and draw. Caelan said he had never injured him but loved him better than he could devise, the best thing anyone says in the whole story, and no one in the square understood it, and Lucan, who was not listening to the words but only to the shape of them, who saw only a Thornwick backing away from an Ashgrave, stepped forward in a rage and cried out against such calm and dishonourable submission.

I have a vision, and I have had it more than once, I am standing in a field at noon and someone presses a sword into my hand, it is cold and perfectly balanced and heavier than it looks, it does not say use me, it says nothing at all, but it is the kind of thing that once you are holding it you cannot imagine how you ever stood in the world without it. That is how it was for Lucan, he was a man made of wit who had never had to prove he was brave because everyone had always known he was clever, he had been waiting his whole life for a quarrel worth dying in and now one had come that was not even his own. He drew, and called Garrick a ratcatcher, and Garrick called him nothing. They fought. Caelan stood between them with his arms out and cried louder than he had ever cried in his life that they should stop, that the prince had forbidden this in the streets of Harrowmere. It was for the third time in the story the right thing, the only thing, for a peacemaker to do, and Garrick under Caelan's arm in a single movement so small and quick that no one saw it drove his blade in. Then he ran.

Lucan stood there and put a hand to his side and looked at it for what seemed a long time, I am hurt, he said, and Merrin said courage man the hurt cannot be much, and Lucan said no it is not so deep as a well nor so wide as a church door but it is enough, it will serve, ask for me tomorrow and you will find me a grave man, he was joking, of course he was joking, he had never in his life said a serious thing and would not begin now. But the joke is the saddest thing in the story, it is the sound of a man who has understood what is happening and has decided to make his last words a little gift for the people he is leaving. Then the laughter went out of him and he said a plague on both your houses, three times. It was a curse and not a curse, it was a diagnosis, for Lucan was neither Thornwick nor Ashgrave, he was kinsman to the prince, a free man, an outsider to the feud. He was the one who died of it, that is what feuds do, they do not stay in the houses where they began, they reach across the wall and take whoever is nearest. He was carried away and was dead before they reached a door.

Caelan stood in the street with his friend's blood on his sleeve and thought, it was the last time he would think clearly for a week, he had one hand open and one hand closed, in the open hand the thing he had said all morning, I am a kinsman now, I will not fight, the feud is over for me. In the closed hand the thing the second voice had been whispering. He had heard it and neither answered it nor refused it, your friend is dead because of you, you stood still, you were gentle, you were soft, is this what love has made of you, a man who watches his friends bleed. It is, Reader, very nearly a perfect lie, built from true parts, it has a dead man in it and a failure and a shame. It ends in a conclusion, therefore kill, which does not follow from any of them. He said aloud that his friend the prince's kinsman had his mortal hurt in his behalf and that the beauty of Aveline had made him soft and had dulled the steel of his valour. He blamed her, it was the first time, only a half sentence in the heat, but a man who blames the woman he loves for his failure to be violent has already begun to become a murderer.

Garrick came back, because he had heard the cry go up and a man cannot run from a deed and stay a man in his own house, he was flushed and not smiling. Caelan said take back the word villain that you gave me, for Lucan's soul is but a little way above our heads waiting for yours to keep him company, either you or I or both must go with him. The swords met. It did not last long, Garrick was the better swordsman by every measure and on any other day he would have won. But he was a man whose whole body had been taught to hate and Caelan was a man whose whole body had been taught to forget it, and when the forgotten thing wakes it is stronger than the practised one, and Garrick fell on his back and looked up at the white sky. The last thing he saw was a young man's face above him and on that face in the first second after the deed was not triumph or rage but a terrible total astonishment.

Merrin was at his side in a moment, away, he said, be gone, the citizens are up and Garrick is slain and the prince will doom you to death if you are taken. Caelan said I am fortune's fool. He ran, and I have thought about that sentence for a long time, fortune's fool, in the hour he became a killer he did not say I am a sinner, he did not say I have done wrong, he said he was the plaything of fate. That is pride's last disguise, the man who says the world did this to me has decided in the same breath that he is not responsible. A man who is not responsible cannot repent, it is the most comfortable lie in the world and it is the one he would carry to the end. The prince arrived with the Ashgraves behind him and Garrick's aunt knelt over the body and cried for blood, not for justice, for the same thing the street had cried for since the first thumb was bitten, and Merrin told the whole truth plainly and in order and it moved neither the old woman nor the prince. But the prince was a just man as far as a man can be in a system that has made him the keeper of a quarrel, he weighed that Garrick had killed Lucan who was his own kinsman and that Caelan had killed Garrick who had killed Lucan. The law was plain and cruel and this once it bent, banishment and not death, mercy but murders, he said, pardoning those that kill. It was the last just thing he would do.

Aveline did not know, she was alone in her room at the top of the house with the curtains drawn waiting for dark and calling to the horses of the sun to hurry. Then the nurse came in weeping and wringing her hands and could not speak, and said only he is dead, we are undone. Aveline believed for one long terrible moment that it was Caelan who was dead. Then the nurse said the name and the truth came out in pieces and what Aveline understood was worse than what she had feared, he was not dead, he had killed her cousin, he had been banished. She cursed him, serpent heart hid with a flowering face. The nurse who had never said a hard word to her in thirteen years said shame come to Caelan. Aveline turned on her and said blistered be thy tongue for such a wish, he was not born to shame, the first time in her life she had defended someone against her own house. She did not cry for Garrick who was her cousin, she cried for the word banished which was worse than ten thousand Garricks dead, and here I will say something I have never said and it may be misunderstood, women excel in the passions of sorrow, sympathy and self and Aveline had them all at once in a single breath and held them without breaking, a man in her position would have drawn a sword, she sat on the floor of her bedroom and felt the whole of it, which is the heavier task. She did it alone, and gave the nurse a ring to carry to him.

He came by the rope ladder in the dark and she was waiting at the top and he came over the sill and took her in his arms and neither of them spoke for a long time, they had one night and nobody in that room was under any illusion about it, at dawn he had to be gone and the law said that if he were found in the city after sunrise he was a dead man, they did not speak of it, they spoke of everything else, and what I want you to hold on to is this, in that room with the lamp turned low and the shutters closed for the length of one night they were not Thornwick and Ashgrave, they were not murderer and kinswoman, they were two people who had made a promise in front of God and had against all odds been allowed to keep it. Then the lark began at the window. She said it was the nightingale and not the lark. He said it was the lark, the herald of the morn. That he must be gone and live or stay and die. She said that the light was not daylight, it was some meteor the sun exhaled, therefore stay, and this is the thing the second voice does best of all, it lets you say the lie yourself, and Caelan, who was no fool and had learned a hard lesson in a day and a half, took the lie into his own mouth and said let me be taken, let me be put to death, I am content if you will have it so. It was she who broke it, saying it is the lark that sings so out of tune, now be gone, more light and light it grows. He said more light and light, more dark and dark our woes.

He went down the ladder and at the bottom he looked up and she stood at the window with her hair loose and in the grey light he thought for the first time that she looked pale. He said to her that he had an ill-divining soul, that he saw her now as one dead in the bottom of a tomb. He said it quietly, he did not know why he said it, but Reader, you know.

The Watchman

The watchman had seen the morning come up on that city ten thousand times and he knew its colours, he knew what it looked like when the light was clean and what it looked like when it had been poisoned by something that had already happened. He watched a young man go over the orchard wall and out through the postern gate and away east toward Eastmarch with his cloak held tight around his shoulders not looking back. He thought of the blade, the same blade in every century, cold and perfectly balanced, making a man feel he had stood in the world before without a purpose, a lie with the weight of truth, and men have always preferred a heavy lie to a light truth, I told you, he said to the east which was already empty, I said it was the best thing that would ever happen to you and the worst, I said do not confuse the two, he did not say it with triumph, he said it the way a man reads a name off a stone, and below him the city began to wake, a bell, a baker opening a shutter. In a small room at the top of a red house a girl stood at a window and watched a road with nothing on it and did not yet know that she had already seen him for the last time alive.

Act IV - The Decree and the Drink

A plan made in fear is a plan made without God, it may succeed, it will not be blessed.

The morning after the banishment the red house did what houses do when a son is dead, it tried to mend itself with a wedding, old Ashgrave was a proud man and in his way a loving one, he saw his daughter weeping and believed he understood, she grieved for her cousin, a girl cannot weep for ever, what she needed was a husband and a household of her own and a reason to lift her face into the light again. He had been speaking with Davin, a good man, young and rich and patient and kind to her at the feast and kinder since, who would not press her and had waited. But the day was short and grief is a door that closes. In a fit of tenderness he mistook for wisdom the old man decided, the wedding would be on Thursday. He asked his wife to tell the girl before she slept, and notice what he was doing, he was arranging his daughter's life the way a man arranges furniture, with care, with affection, with absolute authority, and without once asking her, he was not cruel. That is what made it worse, a cruel father is easy to defy, a loving one who has decided is a wall built out of your own gratitude.

Aveline stood at her window in the dawn after the last night watching an empty road when her mother came in with the news, that early on Thursday morning the noble Davin would make her a joyful bride in the Chapel of the Ridge, and there was a silence in the room. Then she said that he would not, she wondered at such haste, that she must be wed before the man who should be her husband had even come to woo her. It was a small word and an enormous one, it was the first time in her whole life that she had refused anything her parents asked, and also the first thing she had ever said to them that was entirely true.

Old Ashgrave stood in the doorway, for a moment perfectly calm, then the calm broke and what came out of it was the thing that had been sitting beneath every polite word he had ever spoken to her, the whole structure of ownership, the whole old order laid bare, does she not give us thanks, is she not proud, does she not count herself blessed, unworthy as she is, that we have found so worthy a gentleman. She answered that she was not proud of what she hated but thankful even for hate that was meant as love. He shook with rage and called it chopped logic, thank me no thankings nor proud me no prouds, he said, but make ready your fine joints for Thursday or I will drag you there on a hurdle, and Reader, the old word for what had happened in that room is pride. It stood on the wrong side of the argument, it was not Aveline's pride but her father's, a man had made a decision and she had refused to bow to it and what rose in him was not concern for her happiness but the plain furious ego of someone whose will has been crossed, pride begins with I. The I in the doorway said I have decided, I have said, I am the master here.

She knelt and begged him to hear her with patience but one word. He said hang thee, young baggage, disobedient wretch, get to church on Thursday or never look me in the face again, my fingers itch, she turned to her mother and her mother turned away, saying I have done with thee, she turned to the nurse, and this is the cruellest moment in the story and I want you to hold it, the nurse who had loved her for thirteen years and carried her secrets and pushed the rope ladder up the wall herself said what any frightened woman might say who has understood the world, Caelan is banished and all the world to nothing that he dares never come back to claim you, so since the case stands as it does I think it best you married the noble Davin, he is a lovely gentleman, Caelan is a dishcloth to him, it was wisdom, cold and practical and every word of it true. It was the last time Aveline would ask anyone in the world for counsel and receive what she needed, she asked if she spoke from her heart and the nurse said from my soul too. Aveline said amen. That one word quietly was the end of a friendship, she was now alone in the full sense of the word, her father had cast her out, her mother had turned her back, her nurse had failed her, her husband was in exile, and there was one person left in the world and she was going to see him.

The friar's cell was dim and smelled of herbs and he was bent over a table mixing something in a small glass dish, not surprised to see her, for he had been waiting for someone to come, and Davin was there already discussing with the old man the arrangements for Thursday, the soul of courtesy, he did not know, and Reader, understand that Davin was not a villain, he was in every way the story allows a good man in the wrong place who loved a girl who did not love him, and when she came in he said happily met my lady and my wife. She answered with a riddle because the truth was not available, that may be, sir, when I may be a wife. He kissed her hand and left. The friar closed the door. Aveline took out a small knife and told him that unless he could tell her how to prevent this marriage she would help it herself with the blade, God joined her heart to Caelan's and the friar joined their hands and before she would turn to another this knife would slay them both, she was not threatening him, she was not being dramatic, she was telling him with perfect clarity what she was prepared to do and what she was not, she would not be unfaithful to a vow made before God, she would die before she lied at the altar, this was not a child talking, this was someone who had understood perhaps too young the weight of the word married.

And here is the thing about the friar that I have been saving, he had a choice, he could have said child put down the knife, I will go to your father myself and tell him everything, the marriage and the banishment and the vow, it will be a scandal and it will be hard but the truth is a better foundation than a lie and we will bear what we must, that is the road of faith, it is narrow and it leads through shame and it is the way a man takes when he believes that God is not only in the end but in the means. He did not take it, instead he said that he spied a kind of hope which required as desperate an act as the thing it would prevent, and there it was, the clever idea, the second voice which had been quiet since the feast now speaking in the friar's mouth with the friar's gentle professorial calm, you are a man of science, you know the herbs, you can solve this, you do not have to ask anyone, you do not have to trust anyone, only do it yourself, quickly, quietly. It will be as if it never happened, the oldest temptation in the book, the one whispered in the garden and whispered ever since to every man who thinks he can manage the thing himself.

He explained it slowly, he had a drink which he had made himself, a distillation of certain herbs. A person who took it would fall into a sleep so deep and cold and quiet that no man alive could tell her from the dead, no pulse, no breath, no colour, for forty-two hours she would lie as though in the tomb, on Wednesday night she would take it, on Thursday morning they would find her dead and carry her as custom was to the vault of Ashgrave. He would send a letter to Caelan in Eastmarch telling him the whole truth. Caelan would return in secret and be waiting at the tomb when she woke, and together they would go. She did not hesitate for long, not because it was sensible but because it was the only door left, and notice the grammar of the thing, I will send a friar, he said, not I will go, not I will see to it with my own hands, a letter carried by a man who was not the author to a place that was not near in a world where every message depends on something that cannot be controlled, the flaw in the whole design and invisible because the plan was beautiful, for there is a quality of mind I have noticed in people who plan too well, they fall in love with the architecture, they see the whole machine and forget that machines are run by men and men are not gears, they forget the rain and the road and the sickness at a town gate and the one friar in ten who stops to talk, they plan for the world as it ought to be and the world as it is does not forgive them.

She took the vial, small and cold. It sat in her palm like a stone. She went home and knelt to her father and said what she was taught to say, that she was sorry, that she had been wilful, that she had seen the friar and he had shown her the wrong in it and she would be ruled by him in all things, and old Ashgrave was so glad he could not speak, he raised her up and kissed her and said the wedding would be tomorrow, not Thursday, for he could not bear to wait, he sent the servants running and ordered a feast and laughed and wept, and for one moment in the whole of the red house there was joy, built on a lie, and every person rejoicing was rejoicing over a thing that had not happened, which is the final cruelty of the plan, it makes everyone happy and every happiness is a debt.

The night came and she was alone in her room, she had sent away the nurse and her mother and everyone. She sat on the edge of the bed with the vial on the table and the lamp turned low, and now at last the second voice spoke to her, what if it does not work, what if it is poison, what if the friar has been bribed or is afraid or is simply a man who has made a mistake, what if you wake before he comes and find yourself in the dark with the dead, what if you wake and Caelan has not come, what if he has forgotten you, what if you are mad. She was alone with it, no friar in the room, no nurse, no mother, only her own mind telling her every terrible thing she had ever feared. A cold fear thrilled through her veins that almost froze the heat of life. She called for the nurse and stopped herself and said my dismal scene I needs must act alone, and notice that, of everything she says in the whole story it is the sentence I return to most, alone, she did not say without help, she did not say without comfort, she said that she must act this scene alone, it is the moment of the soul before God which each of us will face and in which no other person, no friar, no father, no husband, can stand beside us. She lifted the vial and thought of the tomb and the bones of her ancestors and the body of Garrick lying newly buried in his shroud, and of waking in that place in the dark, and last of all she thought of Caelan. She said I drink to thee, and drank, and lay down on the bed with the curtains drawn, and for a moment there was only the sound of her own breathing, slow and then slower. Then nothing.

The sun came up on a house full of noise, the cooks in the kitchen and the musicians arriving and the old man up since dawn shouting at servants and tasting sauces and trying not to be overheard saying that this was the happiest day of his life. The nurse went up to wake her, calling why lamb, why lady, you slug-a-bed, why bride. She put out a hand and touched her, and her hand came back as if it had touched a flame. She cried that she was dead. The whole house came up the stairs, the father, the mother, the bridegroom with a wreath in his hand. The old man bent and felt her wrist and her forehead and her mouth and said death lies on her like an untimely frost upon the sweetest flower of all the field, the most beautiful thing he had ever said. He said it as an accusation, and Davin did not speak, he stood with the wreath and his hand opened and the flowers scattered on the floor and his whole face came apart, and old Ashgrave began to speak slowly as though remembering a script he had forgotten he knew, all things that we ordained festival turn from their office to black funeral, our wedding cheer to a sad burial feast, our bridal flowers serve for a buried corpse, and all things change to the contrary, he had lost the thing he treasured above everything by his own hand and did not know it, not yet. But the knowledge was already in the room standing behind him, patient. It would wait.

The friar came in grave and said all the right things in a firm voice, peace, for shame, heaven and yourselves had part in this fair maid, now heaven hath all, and all the better is it for the maid. He was lying, and Reader, it is the hardest thing in the story to watch, he was lying to a weeping father and a weeping mother and a man whose happiness he had just destroyed, he told himself it was only for a few hours, he told himself the letter was on its way and the end would be good, he was a man who had stepped off the path and now every step he took carried him further.

The Watchman

The watchman had no words for this part, he had watched the procession leave the red house, the white linen and the flowers and the long train of mourners winding down through the narrow streets to the stone vault at the foot of the hill, he had seen the girl carried in and laid among the dead and the great door pushed shut behind her. He did not weep, he had seen too many funerals. But he did something he had never done before, he turned his face from the city and looked for the first time to the sky, I have said what I could, he said, I have said it in every voice I know, they do not listen and I do not think they can, it is not their fault, it is the nature of the thing, the proud do not hear and the frightened do not hear and the in-love do not hear and the clever do not hear and between them they have filled the world, and somewhere far to the south on a road he could not see a man was riding hard toward Eastmarch with a letter that would never arrive. He said, Lord, the first time in the story he had said the word aloud, I am a watchman and I have watched, I do not ask You to stop what comes, I ask only that when it is over they may understand, and there was no answer, there was only the wind, which had been rising since morning, and which carried the smell of rain.

Act V - The Letter That Never Came

They missed the mark, not by a great distance, by the width of a single day.

It rained for two days, the kind of rain that does not fall so much as settle, grey and patient, on the roofs of Harrowmere and the road to Eastmarch and the fields between, the streams rose and the ditches filled. A traveller on foot in a brown habit with a sealed letter in his sleeve found himself stopped at the gate of a small town where a guard with a lantern looked at him a long time, for the town had the plague, or was feared to have it, a house had been shut up and a neighbour had died. The guard, a decent man doing a hard job, said no one who had been in that house might leave, and Brother Osric, a good simple brother who had gone in only to visit a sick man, was shut inside with the others, he pleaded, he showed the letter, he said it was a matter of life and death for a young man in Eastmarch who must be told. The guard was sorry and could not let him pass, so the letter stayed in a sleeve, and for two days in the dark of a locked house it did not move.

Reader, I want you to see this clearly because it is the part of the story that is always told as an accident, they say what bad luck, they say what a terrible coincidence that the plague should come to that town on that day, but there is no luck in it, there is a man who made a plan that depended on a single messenger on a single road on a single day and who said to himself that God would see to the rest, and God saw to the rest, He allowed the plan to fail, I do not say that He wished it, I say that a plan not offered up cannot be held up. A thing that stands on its own legs falls when the ground moves.

In Eastmarch in a small rented room with one window Caelan had been dreaming, the best dream he had had since the banishment, he dreamed that his lady came and found him dead and breathed such life into his lips with her kisses that he woke and was an emperor. He woke smiling, and for a moment before memory returned he believed it had all been a bad night and that he would go down to the street and find the old city waiting. Then the door opened and his servant Joren stood there in riding clothes covered in mud, he had ridden from Harrowmere through the night on a horse half dead and did not wait to be asked, he said that she was well and nothing could be ill, her body slept in the vault of Ashgrave and her immortal part lived with the angels, he had seen her laid low in her kindred's tomb and had taken post at once to tell it. Caelan did not move. Then he said very quietly I defy you, stars, not a boast, not a curse, the voice of a man who has been struck through the heart and has not yet fallen. He asked if there was a letter from the friar, and Joren said no. He asked again if there was any word, any message at all, and Joren said no my lord, none, and for the first time in all this story Caelan went completely still, like a lamp with no wind.

I should tell you what I think happened in him then because it is the last important thing in his life, he had a choice, he had held for a day and a half a vow in one hand and a grief in the other, he might have gone to the friar's cell, he might have asked and waited and discovered the truth, it would have taken one conversation. But the second voice had been quiet for a long time and it saw its chance and it spoke. It did not sound like a lie, it sounded like his own heart, she is dead, you have lost her, there is nothing left in the world worth having, you have already killed once, you have already failed everyone, there is only one thing left that is yours to decide. He did not argue with it, he did not answer it, he did not pray, he said, well, Aveline, I will lie with thee tonight. That is the sentence, it is the whole of the tragedy, a man in a room with a servant at the door decides to die and does not stop to ask whether the thing that told him to is true.

He walked through Eastmarch with the dull purposeful tread of a man who is no longer in a hurry, he knew the shop, he had passed it before and noticed with the idle eye of a young man who sees poverty and does not feel it a ragged old apothecary with a shop full of dried skins and empty boxes. He said to him that famine was in his cheeks and need and oppression starved in his eyes and contempt and beggary hung upon his back, the world is not thy friend nor the world's law, break it and take this. He held out forty pieces of gold, a great deal of money. The old man hesitated, he knew what it was for and what the law said of it, he looked at the gold and at his own thin hands and said the thing every poor man has said to every rich one since there were coins, my poverty but not my will consents. Caelan said I pay thy poverty and not thy will. The apothecary took the money and gave the poison, and I have thought a long time about this exchange and what I notice is how little either man believed in what he did, Caelan was not evil and the apothecary was not wicked, neither hated anyone, they were two people each driven by a hunger and each reaching toward a thing that would make the hunger stop, neither looking up to see where he stood. That is what sin is in the end for most of us, it is not a great black deed, it is a small transaction between two people who have forgotten who is watching.

He came back to Harrowmere in the dark, his horse worn out and himself on foot by the time he reached the churchyard at the edge of the old city, it was night and cold and the rain had stopped, he carried a crowbar and a torch and the little vial under his coat, and Joren whom he had made wait at a distance watched him go with an uneasy heart, the vault of Ashgrave was a low stone building half sunk in the earth with an iron door, and inside were the bones of three hundred years of one family's dead, and newest of all among them a girl in a white gown lying on her back with her hands folded. But he was not the only one who had come, Davin was already at the door with flowers and a page beside him and a face white and hollow in the torchlight, come in the dark and in secret to scatter flowers on the grave of the girl he had been going to marry. He saw a man with a crowbar at the tomb of the Ashgraves and did not know him, only that it was the banished Thornwick who had murdered his love's cousin, come to do some villainous shame to the dead. He drew.

Caelan had no wish to fight him, he begged him, good gentle youth, tempt not a desperate man, fly hence and leave me, think upon these gone, let them affright thee, put not another sin upon my head by urging me to fury, by heaven I love thee better than myself for I come hither armed against myself, and notice, it was the third time in the story that he refused to fight and the third time the fight was forced upon him, the first time he stood between Garrick and Lucan, the second time he stood still in the square, and now with the poison in his coat and the tomb at his back he stood in front of a man who was only doing what honour required and begged him to go, and Davin did not go. It was over in seconds, and Davin fell and said with the last of his breath the most generous sentence in the story, if thou be merciful, open the tomb and lay me with Aveline. Caelan bent over him and held the torch to his face and saw who it was, and said that he was one writ with him in sour misfortune's book. He opened the door and carried Davin in and laid him down with the bones of the Ashgraves and said nothing more, the only act of grace he performed in the last hour of his life, for a stranger, and no one saw.

She lay on a stone slab with her hands folded and the torch made a small gold light in the dark vault and in it her face was white and her lips faintly blue and she did not seem to breathe, but what he saw was not death, it was beauty as strong as it had ever been. He whispered that death had sucked the honey of her breath and had no power yet upon her beauty. He was wrong. He was in the most terrible way almost right, for she was not dead, she was a few minutes from waking, and if he had only put down the torch and stood very still and watched her chest he would have seen it rise, but Reader, he did not wait, a man who has made a decision does not look for reasons to unmake it, he said eyes look your last, arms take your last embrace, and lips, doors of breath, seal with a righteous kiss a dateless bargain to engrossing death. He kissed her and her lips were very cold and he did not think anything of it. He drew out the vial and looked at it a long time and said here's to my love, and drank, and said O true apothecary, thy drugs are quick, thus with a kiss I die. He fell across her body and lay still.

She woke slowly, like surfacing from a long way down, first the cold, then the dark, then something heavy and warm across her chest and a smell of iron and oil and the wild herbs of the friar's own garden. The old man himself was there at the door with a lantern and a crowbar and a face that had aged ten years in a day, too late, he had heard on the road that something was wrong and had come to find her waking and take her away and tell her it was all arranged. He saw the body of Davin and the body of Caelan and the empty vial and he said come, I will dispose of thee among a sisterhood of holy nuns, stay not to question for the watch is coming, go, good Aveline, I dare no longer stay, he heard voices and he was afraid, he had been afraid for two days, and Reader, I will say something about this man because it is the last time he appears and I do not wish to leave him as only a coward, he loved them, he wanted peace, he did not intend any of this. But he made a plan in secret and the plan broke and when the thing he had made came crashing down the first thing he did was run, it is human, it is only human. That is the whole of the problem.

She would not go with him, she said get thee hence, for I will not away. He left her and went out into the dark and did not come back. She was alone in the vault with the dead. She put out a hand and touched Caelan's lips to see if any of the poison was left. She kissed him, and said thy lips are warm, they were not, they were warm only because she had not yet understood. A moment later they would be colder than stone. She took his dagger and said this is thy sheath, there rust and let me die, she did not hesitate, she was a child and she was not a child, she did what she had always done, which was decide. She lay down beside him.

The watch came with torches and found three bodies in a tomb. The page of Davin who had run for help, and Joren who had waited in terror at the gate. The old friar caught at the gate trembling with tools in his hand. The prince came without his crown, woken in the night, and behind him in a grey dawn that was only now beginning to lighten the east came the two old men, Ashgrave first, who saw the bodies and the dagger and his daughter's white face and the red on her gown and for a long moment did not understand. Then Thornwick, crying, saying that his wife was dead tonight, that grief at his son's exile had stopped her breath, what further woe conspires against mine age. They stood opposite each other across the bodies in the grey light, the two old men who had hated each other for thirty years and could not remember why.

The friar told the whole story plainly and in order and without defence, the marriage, the banishment, the drink, the letter, he did not ask for mercy and did not offer excuses, he said only that if anything in this had miscarried by his fault then let his old life be sacrificed some hour before its time to the rigour of the severest law, the first honest thing anyone had said in the whole of the story. The prince said we have still known thee for a holy man. He turned to the two fathers and said the thing I have been waiting the whole story for someone to say, where are these enemies, Ashgrave, Thornwick, see what a scourge is laid upon your hate, that heaven finds means to kill your joys with love, and I for winking at your discords too have lost a brace of kinsmen, all are punished, and notice the last sentence, he did not say you, he did not point, he included himself, he the prince, the authority, the law, he too had winked, he too had looked away, the only moment in the story in which any man in power takes his share. Then old Ashgrave put out his hand and said O brother Thornwick, give me thy hand, this is my daughter's jointure, for no more can I demand, and Thornwick took it and said but I can give thee more, I will raise her statue in pure gold that while Harrowmere is known by that name there shall be no figure set at such a rate as that of true and faithful Aveline, and Ashgrave said as rich shall Caelan's lie beside his lady, poor sacrifices of our enmity.

There was a peace, the peace the whole city had been trying and failing to make for thirty years. It was made at last standing in the mouth of a tomb over the bodies of their children, real and permanent and it cost everything, and this is what I mean when I say they missed the mark, not that they did not reach it, they reached it, they stood on the exact spot where the whole story had been trying to lead them and shook hands. The hands were old. The hour was late, they arrived, but they arrived after everyone was dead.

The Watchman on the Ridge

The sun came up over Harrowmere on a city that had stopped, the market empty and the shops shut and the street where the first thumb was bitten quiet and the wall of the orchard quiet and in the narrow stone lane that led to the vault a small crowd standing in silence looking at nothing, and on the ridge above the city the watchman stood alone, he had watched all of it, the thumb and the feast, the balcony and the wedding, the blade and the banishment and the drink and the rain and the tomb, he had seen it every time in every century in every city and had said what he could. But he had not left, a watchman does not leave, he stays at the edge of the world on the ridge in the cold looking down, and when the story is over he does not close the book, he waits for the next one. He thought of the two children, not as tragic though they were, not as foolish though they were that too, but as two people who had one chance to be good and who were for a few hours nearly so. He said, Caelan was a man who held a vow in one hand and a grief in the other and let go of the vow, Aveline was a woman who held a vow in one hand and a fear in the other and held on, that is the whole difference between them, it is not the whole of the difference between a man and a woman, but it is something.

Pride begins with I, he said, it does not begin with the proud, it begins with the frightened and the grieving and the in-love and the clever, with anyone who looks at the world and says I will fix this myself, the friar said it, Caelan said it, the fathers said it, the prince who did nothing said it by his silence, and every one of them was a good man, that is the terror, they were not wicked, they were simply alone, each in his own head, each in his own plan, and none of them looked up, I do not say it for blame, I say it so that you will know, if you are ever in a city where two houses hate each other and a young man is in love with a girl on the wrong side of the wall and an old man in a brown robe says he has a plan, do not trust the plan, trust the one who made the plan and ask him to take it to God first, it is slower, it is harder, it is the only thing that works. He turned to go and turned back and said, remember, remember the thumb. The morning light spread across the stones of Harrowmere. The watchman on the ridge stood alone. The city lay open beneath him like a book that has just been shut.

Pride begins with I. So does love. Only one of them ends with we.
