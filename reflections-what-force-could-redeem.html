Reflections: What Force Could Redeem Such a Man?

By The Falconer · 30 October 2026

He sits, looking upon the ruins of what once was, a commander of men, a conqueror of himself—or so he believed. Did the enemy breach his fortress, or had he willingly opened its gates? He cannot remember, though he claims to know the causes of his own destruction.

Reader, imagine a man—he holds one object in his left hand and another in his right. He sits up, staring at them, tears in his eyes. He knows he can only have one at a time. On the left, he holds his physical potential, his ability to dominate and command authority; on the right, he holds his greater, more natural gift: his intellect and ability to live among abstractions.

Virtue through reason, faith, and discipline.

Discipline is the master of greatness; endeavour, the teacher of hope.

Adequacy is the enemy of those who strive for greatness.

The breeze would hit you after a day of thorough use of your muscles; you would be covered in sweat and salt. You would walk around as an Olympian among men. What happened—how did that entity die?

Do you remember how strong you felt? You were a lion, ready to eat all who stood in your way. Did it die when she left, or did it die earlier, internally?

Don’t you remember? Any girl you wanted, you could get; anything you demanded, you could attain. And in the course of that length of time you know as the fall, you forgot yourself and betrayed all you called sacred.

The past is the window through which we view ourselves; we are merely shards of it, scattered parts of what once was.

Did you do something to warrant earthly damnation and humiliation? What made you fall from on high? Why can you not remember where you came from? Can’t you retrace the steps?

Stop! He screams to himself—how did I get here? This was meant to be my great moment? Now I look back on the ruins of what I once was?

It overwhelms him with a sudden bolt of thought—the grand revelation: he has fallen; the man he once was is no more.

Eating foods you said you would never eat, partaking in drinks you would’ve called disgraceful, spending too much time pondering on the dead, engaging in acts you never would’ve before—not only were you betrayed by that girl, but you betrayed yourself and all your values.

Lament is the light that illuminates my lampstand—from it I view the world.

This must surely cause death—living in the shadow of your own grief.

The army disintegrates, the commander looks upon his men, though no longer do they heed his orders. He orders them to stand, yet he himself retreats - the first act of betrayal coming not from the soldiers, but from the one who once commanded them.

What is left is a husk of a man, the man who ran from life. He fled like an army routed in battle—an army running into a cave and sealing it shut.

You were the commander of that army; your men died around you one by one. Before you knew it, you had forgotten your past life, war and conquest, victory and glory. You philosophised and abstracted yourself—you created your own world and forgot about the one you left behind.

The drums of war and the battle cries of the men who fight them are only the foreshadowing of the screams and clanks of Hades.

War is the risk that must be taken for fulfilment.

Don’t be hasty with the sword in battle; you can be struck back.

He enters the cave, believing he sees pleasure—it’s a trap. He is now forced to confront himself; he must investigate his desires—or are they not desires? He does not know—that is the torture of being trapped.

Within the cave he finds another kingdom, its inhabitants not men but thoughts, its battles not physical but metaphysical. He rejoices, believing himself free from the affairs of mortals, yet what has he conquered? He commands his thoughts, or do his thoughts command him?

Philosophy is where the men of the physical world run away from their problems; they run into the abyss of the metaphysical.

The words of the mind are more comfortable than the words of the physical.

He observes his days; he writes; the devil whispers in his ear—philosophy indeed.

He himself wants to be asked questions but hides from answers to his own. He speaks in riddles, a Gnosticism in which only he has the knowledge—knowledge of his own world.

He toils, yet he does not work; he labours, yet bears no fruit; he rests but is exercised—his life is hubris, living between the winds of life.

You let one thought in; one thousand thoughts march behind it, overriding your rationality.

Why does one thought lead to another? Can’t you pull yourself in?

Passion overwhelms reason, and reason introduces passion.

Hedonism is my master; pleasure, desire, degeneracy—whip me into submission. The woe of the primal man, the woe of him from whom virtue and discipline have fled.

My passions rage in me: paranoia, introversion, anxiety. At any moment, I feel I could spontaneously combust; at any moment, what did he mean by this or that? Is he out for me?

Don’t believe so. I genuinely do see myself as separate persons in one body. That part of me is a genuine other person who sneers at me. He is a dark figure; I look back at him with a blaze of suspicion.

The halls of the conscience become crowded, thoughts assemble as armies - each proclaiming itself the rightful ruler. He observes them, investigates them, yet never enters the battle; perhaps the commander has become a spectator to the destruction of his own kingdom.

The devil tells you, “You’ve been awake for five hours; don’t you think it’s time to rest?” He makes you focus on your past. Notice: he begs to blind you to your future.

The devil will twist good into bad. He says, “Go to the monastery, forget studying.” Notice: when you actually have time for the monastery with intention, he begs you not to.

The devil whispers in your ear, “Can’t you see? Only you have these thoughts and can write these things!” The enemy wants you to become fat with pride; when convenient, they will slaughter you like a pig.

You thought you could deceive God; He handed you over to your own foundations, faulty as they are. You fell through.

You ridicule yourself, investigating every discrepancy, looking for the answer—you will never get it; you only lead yourself on in the chase for your past.

Man seeks refuge from his worries; he cannot seek refuge from himself. In this, he pleads with reality, trying to attach himself to external things.

He waits, hoping one will find him—no one comes.

He sleeps, though even in sleep his mind refuses him peace. The world he fled returns in another form - the commander stands before judgement, but whose judgement is it? His enemies’, God’s, or merely that of his own conscience?

Looking above, the blade so imposing—is this the end? I close my eyes and wake up. The fables of my mind have run rampant again. I open my eyes: no blade, no crowd of haughty onlookers—just silence, the reflective examination of isolation. Have I passed this trial? Or has the nightmare of hell just begun?

He leaves the cave, encountering a monk by the river, a man who appears to possess that which the commander seeks. Yet he cannot determine what it is - wisdom, humility, or merely the absence of those passions which have enslaved him?

The monk lives by the river. Laypeople approach him: “So humble you are, oh monk.” Instinctively, the laypeople know that they lack what the monk has. They recognise something in another without having it in themselves—we all possess parts of the One, though to different extents; we all emit from the One.

All things happen in providence; the Logos binds even the strongest-willed men. We have our plans, yet Providence has His.

He spends his days ruled by destiny—or does providence not bind him?

Remember: what you call wisdom is nothing before God.

God is the chisel, I am the stone. Inwardly He carves me; outwardly I carve myself.

Hard times give opportunities to become principled and examine yourself even more so than good times.

Better to be a suffering servant than a successful slave.

Patience is a virtue, a virtue of prudence, one that, when followed, enables swiftness and self-obedience.

It is not what I do, but what you will do. One cannot lead the blind, but the blind must open his own eyes.

Reality represents itself; our feelings attempt to extrapolate it.

How can we retract ourselves from this world of feeling and emotion? Are we not a symphony of feeling and emotion? Would that be to separate from ourselves? But if we do not separate, are we attached? Do not fall into false dichotomies.

To know when you are emotional, you must have something non-emotional to attach yourself to—can you only know one thing in the absence of another?

We want solace in finality rather than the twisted temptations of uncertainty.

Aphorisms, proverbs, tall tales—how easy they are to proclaim, how tenuous they are to acclaim.

The man departs the river, carrying the same body, the same mind, yet with another question - has he attained wisdom, or merely acquired new words with which to explain his failure? The monk returns to his solitude, the commander to his duties, though only one appears content with what he possesses.

It appears you write only when you are transitioning or need to—make it subconscious to reflect daily.

You tell yourself you will do something; you even admit you should not do it. Then you forget what you said and do something else.

After he is fed and has been given two days of meagre food, he betrays his discipline and returns to the pen, selling himself short. Happy with his unseen shortcoming, he smiles as he enters the embrace of the pigpen.

Straying from the tracks of God, I claim to miss the train, but only missed the mark, missed myself; therefore, missed You, God.

Be mindful of yourself; watch yourself. Are you only looking to God when you are weak? Do you forget Him when victory is granted? Do you run through the gates of the city and forget to look back at who sent you?

Don’t get caught up in your own reflections—you depend on God.

You are hungry, you are restless—go pray, face the east.

Life feels most dear alone; you notice more, and indeed must—as soon as you associate with others, their plebeian noise interferes with your thoughts.

Better to be alone and sharp than surrounded and dull.

The commander remembers his former army, the banners, the conquests, the victories - all now relics of a world he cannot return to. He seeks restoration, yet each attempt begins with contemplation and ends in the same place. Can one command an army from within a cave?

He has waited for this day, the day he would finally regain his inspiration.

You sit there and waste your day.

Your competitors are not.

Do it now, return to victory.

You wake up and instantly think of how to look impressive to others. This is wrong—correct yourself.

You exaggerate your life since you have not built anything noteworthy in it—conquer and attain treasure; perhaps with the spoils of war you could speak.

Walk in: the first and only thing—think of your task. Only gaze at what will benefit you; ignore all that will not.

How foolish it is to spend your last reserves. Don’t you know you’ll need them when the time comes?

When you enter a new environment, be wary. You may think new faces thus mean a new life and hence greater social interaction—don’t fool yourself. Nothing is new under the sun; if you yourself have not changed, your surroundings will not. Most people do not change themselves in new environments; they merely reveal different parts of their character.

Hiding under the crevice of false duty presses the issue forward but not away.

Moments are temporary and fleeting; avoidance lasts longer.

Do not wait until the armies have assembled on the field; your conscience knows when the march has begun—run to the fortress, return to proverbs of old, retract from conflict, make sure it never happens.

The devil throws all tricks at you; he begs for your destruction—do not let foul words with a liar bring you back to despair.

Don’t let the crows peck at your remains; if necessary, let them fly behind you, willingly use them in battle.

Peace, peace—how easily the word is spoken. But am I to give up all that I possess within myself?

Running makes the truth slip further from you; accepting reveals what will always be revealed.

He stands before the entrance, looking upon the world which he abandoned. The mind tells him to return, the body remains still - perhaps the armies of his conscience have not yet reconciled themselves. How strange it is, a commander who once feared no mortal now hesitates before the ordinary affairs of life.

Damaged, scarred, fractured—can you ever repair, or can you merely cover up the past?

Am I the victor, or have I merely held out the longest?

What force could redeem such a man and allow him to exit the cave—surely not himself?

Alas, the commander stands between two kingdoms, one he has lost and another he has imagined - neither offering him the certainty he so desperately seeks. Will providence raise him from his ruins, or has he mistaken another moment of inspiration for the restoration of himself? He looks towards the world, though whether he shall march forward or retreat once more, even he cannot know.
