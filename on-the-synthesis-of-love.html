On the Synthesis of Love

By The Falconer · 24 November 2026

Love is a will before it is a feeling: desire ordered by reason, the understanding of another, and a debt that outlives the beloved.

Preface

Humanity since the dawn of time has struggled with the question of what love is, and has just as long failed to agree on the answer. One may wonder why the word “synthesis” is placed in the title; to be clear, it is because love is not one thing but many things gathered into one, a will, a desire, an act of understanding and a debt owed to God, and because the author’s own writings, set down over years and in very different states of mind, were all saying the same thing without his knowing it. The author does not claim to have loved well; he claims only to have watched it closely, as one watches a fire from across a river, warmed by it, scorched by it, and honest enough to report both.

No system is offered here. What is offered is a synthesis: the gathering of many separate things the author has written about love into the one thing they were always saying. To summarise the position, love is a will before it is a feeling, it is desire ordered by reason, it is the understanding of another from their own point of view, and it outlives the one it was given to.

I. What love is

It is easiest to begin with what love is not. The argument proceeds by three denials and one affirmation.

1. Love is not want. The author once wrote that when someone is a wanter they will go low for their wants, and that the wanting runs them straight into the arms of desolation; eventually people see through, cut off, and those who remain have malicious intent and are playing a long game, waiting for desperation, and they lash out as sharks do when an animal is bleeding. Hunger asks only what can be taken, and it has never once asked who is there.

2. Love is not loneliness. The question has been put plainly: does he long for her or simply think of her, does he desire her or does she merely fill a place, is he love-bound or merely lonely and in need? The test is a cruel one, for if the person is removed and the longing remains it may be love, and if only the empty place remains, where anyone might have stood, it was never about them. The journey is the answer.

3. Love is not sympathy. Sympathy is an excellent feeling but, when disordered, it is often wasted, and the better route is understanding, for empathy means to understand someone from their point of view. There is a great difference between the man who looks into a human and the man who looks with one. The author lived in the first posture, reading everyone and pursuing whatever he wished with ruthlessness, and it cost him every real connection he had; to read a person is not to love them.

4. Love is a will before it is a feeling. “My will to love you is here,” the author wrote, and the line stands though the one it was written to is gone. Feeling comes and goes with the weather of the day, and the will is what remains once the weather has passed.

The definition follows from these four. Love is the decision to understand another from their own point of view and to hold them dear after learning what there is to learn.

II. Love and desire

At first glance one may say that desire is the enemy of love, and that the body ought to be left out of it. On rebuttal one would point to the nature of man, who is both heart and mind: the heart is eager, raging with passions and desire, while the mind sits still, yearning to establish the rule of the logos in oneself, and nevertheless the desires march forward, haughty in nature and bowing to no one. Passion overwhelms reason, and reason introduces passion, and so they are not two armies but two halves of one motion.

The author has argued that there is a point at which two people reach what he calls the ultimate physical interaction, where the physical passes into the metaphysical and the consciousness that connects two as one is no longer diluted by the interception of reality, and that once two have become one the connection holds by conscience and does not need constant touch. This is why love cannot be only the body and also why it cannot be without it, for the flame must be lit before the question it burns for can be answered.

But desire disordered is a poison, and it has two counterfeits. The first is hedonism, which the author once named as his master: pleasure, desire and degeneracy whipping him into submission, which is only the original lie of the devil, that one can do whatever one wants, dressed as freedom. A man who believes it never reaches the metaphysical; he reaches the pig pen and calls it an embrace. The second is obsession. It is true imprisonment to be obsessed with one, love not needed nor nurtured, chains as a slave sees bracelets. Obsession dresses as devotion, but it asks nothing of the beloved and nothing of the self except that the chain stay fastened. Love must be nurtured; obsession need only be fed.

III. Love and God

The argument of this section is that love does not begin in man. It is given through the loving gift of the Son, by whom mankind receives perfect mercy, justice and love, and every love between men is a small reflection of that, so that learning to love is learning to stand in the right relation to the One from whom it flows. This is why the three loves cannot be separated. The author, straying from the tracks of God, claimed to miss the train, but had only missed the mark, and so missed himself, and therefore missed God. To miss God is to miss oneself, and the man who has missed both has nothing true to give another. God is the chisel and man is the stone; inwardly He carves him, outwardly he carves himself.

This is also why love needs an order and a house. Virtue comes through reason, faith and discipline. In the home the author imagines, the father is the protector and the giver of wisdom, the mother is the epicentre of grace, love and compassion, and the children are encouraged to question authority respectfully, using logic and the revelation drawn from the natural world God made. He has held that women excel in the passions of sorrow, sympathy and self, and men in brotherhood, honour and order, and he does not say one is greater than the other, only that love is a body of many parts and each part must keep its place for the whole to stand. Love also widens beyond the home, for men are a people, a proud one, and the love of one’s country, rightly held, is not hatred of the stranger but the refusal to watch what one’s fathers built dissolve without a word. All men possess parts of the one, though to different extents, and all emit from the one; and if that is so, to love any part is to love a reflection of the whole.

IV. Love and loss

Love is proved at the grave. It is easy to hold someone dear while they stand in front of you, and the real test comes when they are gone and the holding has nothing left to hold. The author has felt it twice over, and one of them he never met; he was saddened, he loved the kid, and a love can attach to one who never drew breath in the world and be no less love for that. The other he did meet, and he wrote to her: years after your passing melancholy reigns tyrannically, my will to love you is here, your will to resurrect is not, long nights with you, longer eternity without you.

Grief, then, is love with nowhere to go. It is the will still standing and the beloved no longer there to receive it. He has written that living in the shadow of one’s own grief must surely cause death, and he has also written the answer, for lament has a use: lament is the light that illuminates the lamp stand, and from it the world is viewed. Grief rightly kept is a lamp and not a grave.

There is guilt too, which is grief’s harder sibling. The two went from lovers, to partners, to strangers, and then to the great boundary between the living and the dead, and the author saw in it how one sin leads to a multitude of more sins. He did not love her well, and he has asked how he is meant to honour her in this world and meet her in the next. He does not have the answer, but he knows that regret is also a form of love, because it measures what should have been given by the size of what was withheld. It cannot be taken back, and he has learned that it need not be: what God has taken, no man can take back. The one who was loved is placed beyond the reach of every enemy, including the lover’s own failures.

The question that remains is the one put to the clouds, whether one can love again, anew, if one has loved once before, or whether all things are replications of the past. It is not so. Every experience provides the skills necessary for a second chance, whether it be with the same person or someone of similar trait. The past is a window through which a man views himself, but it must not become a wall, and the devil uses the past to distract a man from his future and his present.

V. The effects of love

What, then, does love do to a man? Six effects can be observed.

1. It warms and it exposes. The lover holds the other dear, yet is held at arm’s reach, brought into warmth and left out in the cold, and to love is to hand someone the power to leave one in the cold. The man who will not take that risk is safe and is not loved.

2. It humbles. It draws one down to the human realm, where pride says the lover is above the common run and love shows him he is a man among men.

3. It hardens when it is wounded and not tended. The author took that hardness for wisdom, though he has since written the correction, that the wall protects nothing worth the keeping.

4. It destroys when it is disordered. The author destroyed someone and now knows reality.

5. It clarifies. Hard times give opportunity to become principled and to examine oneself even more than good times do.

6. It gives victory its meaning. He won but there was no one left to share his victory with, and against that stands the small hope in the dark: tonight there is only one star out, and with you it could be two.

VI. The synthesis

Gather the fragments and they say one thing.

1. Love is a will, and it outlasts the feeling. It persists when the beloved is absent, hostile or dead, which is how it is known from want and from loneliness.

2. Desire is its fuel and its danger. Under the logos it becomes union, while disordered it becomes chains that look like bracelets.

3. Love is understanding, the refusal to look into a person as a specimen and the decision to look with them and to stay.

4. Love is vertical before it is horizontal. It flows from God to man and from man to another, and he who misses the first misses himself and then the other, which is why it needs a house and a people to keep it.

5. Love outlives its object, so that grief is love with nowhere to go and honour is the shape love takes after death.

6. Its last duty is to the living. The dead are kept by God and by memory, but the present is still man’s to tend. He is in pain and wants to rest, but he knows he must endure, such is life.

The author began by saying he does not claim to have loved well, and ends by saying that this treatise is itself a kind of love: the will to understand, to honour and to be truthful about what was felt and failed at. Does he long for her or simply think of her? He still does not fully know. But as the old adage goes, the journey is the answer.
