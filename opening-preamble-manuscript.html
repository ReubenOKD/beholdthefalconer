Opening Preamble: Manuscript

By The Falconer · 5 November 2026

Reader, imagine a man - he sits before his desk, surrounded by the writings of men who have long since departed this earth, men whose bodies have returned to dust, yet whose thoughts continue to command the attention of the living.

Before him lie several pages, upon one he has drawn a triangle, points representing the positions of two people within a room.

How strange, that a man who has spent so much time investigating the greatest questions of existence should now find himself investigating the movement of a woman from one chair to another.

He looks at the figure.

He remembers her sitting beside him, he remembers her smile, the movement of her eyes, the conversations which followed. Yet what has happened since? The two who once sat beside one another now appear as strangers, each occupying their own world.

Alas, what has become of him?

He came to university seeking knowledge, yet finds himself investigating a woman whose thoughts remain completely unknown to him.

Surely this is foolishness?

But were his observations not real? Did these things not occur before his very eyes? Is he to deny reality merely because his interpretation of it causes him suffering?

He sits back, his thoughts begin conversing among themselves, each attempting to establish authority over the other.

Reason demands evidence.

Passion demands reassurance.

Pride refuses humiliation.

The conscience, having watched the entire affair unfold, asks whether any of these rulers have the interests of their master at heart.

One thought enters, a thousand march behind it - the mind has become an assembly of competing authorities, none willing to surrender its claim to truth.

He is fatigued.

He turns towards the writings of the ancients, believing that in their words he may find refuge from his own.

Yet what refuge is philosophy to the man who uses it to flee from reality?

He closes his eyes.

A figure appears before him, an elderly man, dressed in garments unfamiliar to the modern world. His face is stern, his appearance dignified, and his gaze that of one who has spent many years examining the follies of men.

He approaches the desk.

It is Plato.

The philosopher examines the books, the scattered pages and finally the triangular figure.

“What is this?” he asks.

“A representation of reality.”

“Of all reality?”

“No, of an observation.”

“And what have you observed?”

“The position of two persons, and the movement of one between places.”

“Then why have you drawn a figure which appears to concern more than their positions?”

I become agitated.

“Do you believe I have invented what occurred? I saw these things myself. The woman sat in one place, she gave her reason for moving, and she moved elsewhere. What fault can you find within the observation?”

“None,” he responds.

“Then what is your contention?”

“That you have mistaken the certainty of an event for the certainty of its meaning.”

Alas, how irritating is the philosopher who attacks an argument which has yet to be fully presented.

I rebuke him.

“Must we not use reason to extrapolate from the world around us? How else are we to understand the intentions of others?”

He looks towards the figure.

“Tell me, if the woman had remained seated, what would you have concluded?”

“That she was comfortable where she sat.”

“And because she moved?”

“That there may have been another reason.”

“May have been?”

“Yes.”

“Then why do you conduct yourself as though there certainly was?”

I remain silent.

The philosopher continues.

“You have constructed a world from what you know, and furnished the rest with what you suspect. You then enter this world, examine its contents, and call your conclusions knowledge.”

I look towards the triangle.

Perhaps he is correct.

Or perhaps I am merely yielding before the authority of a great philosopher, as the weaker man bends before one of greater reputation.

I ask him, “Are you saying that all human relations must remain unknowable?”

“Have I said that?”

“No.”

“Then why do you argue against it?”

Alas! Even in my defence of reason, my own reasoning appears to betray me.

The philosopher walks towards the bookshelf and withdraws a manuscript.

Its pages are worn, its contents unfamiliar.

“Here,” he says, “is the account of a man who sought to investigate another, yet in the course of his investigation became the very thing which required examination.”

I take the manuscript.

“Will this tell me what she thought?”

“No.”

“Will it tell me whether something existed between us?”

“No.”

“Then what purpose does it serve?”

He responds, “You have demanded knowledge of another while refusing to understand the disorder within yourself. Must every question lead you away from the man who asks it?”

I lower my eyes.

Upon the first page is written:

THE THIRD POINT

On the uncertainty of a woman, the pride of the heart, and the tyranny of interpretation.
