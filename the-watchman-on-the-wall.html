The Watchman on the Wall

By The Falconer · 7 October 2026

A man guards a city from everything except the one thing he cannot stop watching.

Reader, imagine a man - he stands on the wall of a city that no longer has enemies. The wars were won long ago, the gates have rusted open, the trumpets hang silent in the armoury, yet every evening he climbs the stair and takes his post. No one has ordered him to. He has ordered himself. A man must guard something, he says - and if there is no enemy outside, he will find one within.

Below the wall runs a path to the well. At the hour the sun leans on the rooftops, a girl walks it with a jar on her shoulder. She does not look up. Why would she ? Walls are not looked at - they are only walked beside.

He looks down.

*

The first evening it was nothing - a figure on a path, a jar, a shadow lengthening. The second evening he noticed the jar was blue. The third, he noticed she walked faster going down than coming back, as if the water were heavier than the emptiness. By the tenth evening he knew the sound her sandals made on the third stone, which was loose, and he waited for it the way a priest waits for the bell.

Notice: he did not decide to watch her. He decided only to keep his post. The watching arrived on its own, as all slaveries do - quietly, dressed as a duty.

He tells himself he is guarding the city. He is guarding a path.

*

He never learns her name. He gives her one, then takes it back, for a name personifies a thing, and he does not wish her to become a person - a person can refuse you. A figure on a path cannot.

So he builds her. From the jar, from the hour, from the way she pauses at the bend to shift the weight - he builds a woman. He gives her a mind that reads what he reads. He gives her a sorrow that mirrors his own. He gives her a reason for never looking up, and the reason is always him: she knows he is there, she is too proud, too shy, too wise, she is waiting for him to descend.

He is the architect of a city of one, and he has made her its only citizen.

*

Reader, you will ask - why does he not simply come down ? The stair is right there. Forty steps, the old soldiers counted, worn smooth in the middle by a century of boots. He could take them in a minute. He could stand by the well when she comes and say anything - good evening, the water is cold, your jar is a beautiful colour. Any word would do. Any word would break the spell, which is precisely why he does not say one.

On the wall he is a watchman. On the path he would be a man - and a man can be seen, and weighed, and found wanting.

He calls it honour - a man of my station does not chase water-girls down a stair. He calls it patience - all things happen in providence, if it is meant she will look up. He calls it wisdom, he calls it restraint, he calls it the discipline of the old philosophers, and he has a hundred names for it, and not one of them is its true name, which is fear.

*

In the night the devil climbs the stair. He is not a beast, he has no horns - he is an old comrade from the wars, and he leans on the parapet beside the watchman like a friend.

"She looked up today" the devil says. "Did you not see ? At the bend, the smallest glance."

The watchman did not see it. He has been watching for a hundred evenings and never once seen it. But now, having been told, he remembers it - he remembers it vividly - a tilt of the head, a flicker of attention, meant for him.

"Go down tomorrow" the devil says. "She is waiting."

And the watchman lies awake with the glance that never happened, polishing it like a medal.

The next night the devil comes again, and this time his voice is cold. "She has never looked up. She does not know you exist. To her you are a stone in the wall - and who would love a stone ?" And the watchman, who an hour ago was a lover, is now a fool, and the shame of it burns the back of his neck.

Reader, notice the trick - both voices kept him on the wall. Hope said wait, she is coming. Despair said wait, there is no point. Neither one said: go down.

*

Seasons turn on the city. The figs ripen and drop and are trodden into the path. He learns the girl by the weather - in the heat she walks slowly, in the rain she runs, and once in winter she slips at the third stone and his whole body leans over the parapet as if he could catch her from forty steps above. She rises, brushes her knee, gathers the jar. She did not need him. She never needed him.

He writes that night - for he writes, the watchman, by a small lamp in the guardhouse, aphorisms no one will read. He writes: it is true imprisonment to love what you will not approach. He reads it back and feels wise. Then he climbs the stair again at dawn to see if she has come early.

Knowledge has never once saved him. A man can describe his chains perfectly and still wear them.

*

He begins to pray about her. Not for her - about her. He kneels in the guardhouse facing the east, and in the place where the icon is, her shape arrives - the jar, the bend, the blue. He tries to put God back in that place, and the shape returns, patient as water. He has built an idol without lifting a chisel. He has made a god of a girl who has never once looked at him, and he bows to it twice a day, at the hour she goes down and the hour she comes back.

What a strange religion - its god does not know she is one, and its only worshipper keeps the temple locked from the inside.

*

One night he dreams he has come down. The forty steps pass under him like nothing, the path is warm, the well is deep and black and smells of old stone. She is there, the blue jar resting on the lip of the well, and he speaks - he cannot hear what he says, only that it is perfect, that it is everything he has composed on the wall for a year. And she turns to him.

Her face is not the face he built. It is plainer, and stranger, and tired about the eyes. She does not read what he reads. She does not carry his sorrow. She looks at him the way one looks at a stranger who has spoken too warmly - politely, waiting for him to finish, already turning back to the water.

He wakes before dawn with his heart beating like the drums of war. And here is the darkest thing, Reader - he is relieved. Relieved it was only a dream. Relieved she is still up there in his mind, unspoiled - the blue jar, the bend, the glance that never happened. He would rather keep the woman he made than meet the one who exists.

He climbs the stair and takes his post, and when she passes beneath him at the hour, he loves her more than ever - for now he knows what it would cost to be wrong.

*

Then one evening the path is empty.

The sun leans on the rooftops at the proper hour. The third stone waits, loose, for its sound. Nothing. He tells himself she is sick, delayed, the well is crowded. The next evening, nothing. On the seventh evening another girl walks the path with a red jar, laughing with a friend, and she is nobody.

He never learns what happened. Perhaps she married. Perhaps her family moved to another quarter, another city, another country across the sea. Perhaps she simply found a nearer well. It does not matter. What matters is that she had a life, a whole life, with rooms and voices and mornings he would never see - and in all of it, he had been nothing. Not a lost chance, not a regret, not a name half remembered. Nothing. A wall she walked beside.

She will never know she was watched. She will never know she was worshipped. She will never know that for a year a man stood above her each evening and built a city in her honour, and let no one into it - least of all her.

*

He keeps his post. What else is there ? The wall is still there and so is he. He climbs the forty steps each evening, looks down on an empty path, and tells himself he is guarding the city.

Sometimes, near the hour, he hears a sandal on the third stone and his heart leaps like an animal in a trap. It is never her. It is the wind, or a child, or the stone settling on its own.

He has learned one thing, and he wrote it down: the watchman who will not leave the wall is not guarding the city - he is its prisoner, the wall is his cell, and he holds the only key.

Reader, I would like to tell you that he came down at last. That one evening he took the forty steps, walked the path himself, drew water from the well, felt how heavy the jar was, and understood her.

But you know him. You have stood on walls of your own. The heart is haughty - it believes it can wait out fate.

The path is empty. The jar is gone. And still, at the hour, the watchman leans over the parapet, looking down at nothing, waiting for someone to look up.
