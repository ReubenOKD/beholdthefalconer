How Can Smart People "Fall For" Religion or the Right?

By The Falconer · 29 October 2026

The idea that being religious or right-wing is somehow tied to being unintelligent or ignorant is a relatively new development — largely a product of the past 30 years. It is not a historical norm, and it certainly is not an intellectual truth.

For the majority of human history, many of the smartest people were deeply religious and, by extension, socially conservative. Religion was not seen as the enemy of reason, but as the framework through which reason operated. Philosophy, ethics, law, science, and governance were all shaped within religious worldviews, not in opposition to them.

Many of the universities that modern progressives now pride themselves on attending were founded by Christians. These institutions were originally built on the assumption that truth, morality, and reason were interconnected. Even many of the values progressives hold dear today — empathy, compassion, kindness, charity, and respect for human dignity — were propagated and systematised by Christianity long before they were secularised.

Yet this historical lineage is often forgotten. There is a widespread assumption that modern society emerged despite religion rather than through it. As a result, religious belief is frequently dismissed as a primitive holdover, and conservatism is framed as resistance to progress rather than as a form of historical memory.

What is rarely confronted is the alternative. When religion is not simply replaced with pluralism or humility, but aggressively removed and substituted with ideology, the results can be catastrophic. Truly secular states that attempted to erase religion entirely — such as the Soviet Union or Communist China — did not produce utopias of reason and compassion. They produced some of the worst atrocities of the modern era, justified not by God, but by the state, the party, or "historical necessity."

This does not mean that atheism itself causes violence, nor that religion is immune to abuse. It means that human beings do not stop believing when religion disappears — they simply redirect belief elsewhere. When transcendent moral limits are removed, power fills the vacuum.

Smart people do not "fall for" religion or conservatism. Many arrive at them through reflection, history, and scepticism of ideological certainty. Conservatism, at its core, is not about rejecting change, but about questioning whether new ideas improve on institutions that evolved for reasons often forgotten.

The modern habit of equating intelligence with secular progressivism is less a mark of insight than of cultural conformity. It flatters those who hold it, but it explains very little about history, human nature, or the foundations of the society we currently inhabit.

Intelligence alone does not guarantee wisdom. Without humility, historical awareness, and moral restraint, it often produces nothing more than confidence without limits — and history has shown where that can lead.
